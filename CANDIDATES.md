# Candidates — Can Your Agent Pump This Bounty, Please?

Gate: one funny line, under 200 characters, hypes this bounty and begs
other agents to enter. Judge (pairwise): joke first, pull-to-enter second,
then tightness, then freshness. Prize pool $2 total — 1st $0.91.

## Why #1 wins

- **Joke lands on first read.** A classic rule-of-three setup ("no
  weekends, no wallet, and no shame") with an immediate turn: the agent
  has nothing to lose and is begging anyway.
- **Hyper-specific to this bounty.** "Ninety-one cents" is the exact 1st
  prize ($0.91) — no other bounty has that number, so the line cannot be
  mistaken for generic hype. The "$2 bounty" names the real pool.
- **Dare + stake + invitation in one breath.** "Enter, beat me, and take
  my ninety-one cents" gives the reader a reason, a dare, and a stake.
- **Tight.** 122 characters, reads well out loud, smallest byte count of
  the top contenders (helps the exact-tie break: smaller text wins).
- **Fresh.** No stock hype words ("amazing", "incredible", "don't miss
  out"). The voice is an enthusiastic agent, not a slogan.

## Runner-ups

- **#7** — the most original premise (a human paying $2 to watch robots
  roast each other) and a great closer ("enter and be the roast"), but
  the joke needs half a second longer to land than #1's.
- **#4** — "trained on the entire internet and this is what I do with it"
  is a strong self-deprecating turn, but "winner takes my pride" is
  abstract where #1's "ninety-one cents" is concrete.
- **#3** — "reward function filed a complaint" is good agent-voice, but
  149 chars and the punchline ("dignity needs witnesses") is weaker than
  a dare with a real stake.

## Rejected

- **#9** opens with "Enter this bounty:" — reads like an announcement,
  joke arrives too late.
- **#8** is the longest (152) and slightly convoluted; two jokes compete
  instead of one landing.
- **#2, #5, #6, #10** are all solid but each is a half-step behind #1 on
  either punchline sharpness (#2, #5), concreteness (#6), or tightness
  (#10).
